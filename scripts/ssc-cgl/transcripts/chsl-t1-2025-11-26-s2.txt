difficulty: EASY
answer: C
stem:
Change the following sentence into the Active form.
The presentation has been given.
A: The presentation was given.
B: They had given the presentation.
C: Someone has given the presentation.
D: Someone gives the presentation.
solution:
The passive "has been given" is present perfect with no agent, so the active uses an indefinite subject and keeps the present perfect: "Someone has given the presentation." "Had given" and "gives" change the tense, and "The presentation was given" is still passive.

chapter: Grammar
subtopic: Direct and Indirect Speech
difficulty: MODERATE
answer: C
stem:
Choose the option that is the most appropriate conversion of the following sentence into Direct Speech.
He told her that the documents had already been signed before she had called.
A: He said, "The documents were already signed before you called."
B: He said to her, "The documents have already been signed before you had called."
C: He said to her, "The documents had already been signed before you called."
D: He told her, "The documents have already been signed before you have called."
solution:
"Told her" becomes "said to her", and "she" becomes "you". Of two past actions the earlier one keeps the past perfect ("had already been signed") and the later one is simple past ("before you called"). The first option drops "to her" and the sense of the earlier action, and the others pair a present perfect with "before you (had/have) called", which is ungrammatical.

difficulty: HARD
answer: C

notice: SSC's provisional key calls this an adverbial clause of place, but a "where" clause that describes the noun "scenarios" is an adjective (relative) clause, so the answer shown is ours.
note: SSC key A (adverbial clause of place) is wrong: the clause qualifies "scenarios". Ours kept.
context:
Read the passage and answer the following questions:
In an economy increasingly defined by automation and volatile market dynamics, the pertinence of skill-based education has never been more conspicuous. Traditional pedagogy, with its emphasis on rote memorisation and theoretical abstraction, often fails to equip learners with the pragmatic competencies demanded by contemporary industries. This schism between academic instruction and workplace exigencies has engendered a workforce proficient in qualifications but deficient in adaptability.

Skill-based education, conversely, prioritises applied learning, fostering dexterity in problem-solving, communication, and technological fluency. Apprenticeships, vocational training, and project-based curricula immerse students in authentic scenarios where knowledge is not merely recited but operationalized. Such experiential learning cultivates not only technical acumen but also critical soft skills indispensable in collaborative environments.

Furthermore, skill-oriented systems encourage a mind-set of lifelong learning, essential in an era where obsolescence can render a profession archaic within a decade. They democratise opportunity by enabling individuals from diverse socio-economic backgrounds to access employment through demonstrable capability rather than academic prestige alone.

Ultimately, integrating skill-based frameworks into mainstream education is not a peripheral reform but a systemic imperative. It reconciles the persistent dichotomy between learning and livelihood, ensuring that education is not an inert archive of facts but a dynamic instrument for personal agency and national progress.

The phrase "where knowledge is not merely recited but operationalized" functions as:
A: Adverbial clause of place
B: Noun clause
C: Adjective clause
D: Absolute phrase

The clause has its own subject and verb ("knowledge is ... recited ... operationalized"), so it is a clause, not an absolute phrase. It comes right after the noun "scenarios" and describes which scenarios are meant ("scenarios in which knowledge is operationalized"), so "where" works as a relative adverb and the clause is an adjective (relative) clause.
